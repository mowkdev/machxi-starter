So the reset now also **removes every federated identity of the account** in the transaction that writes the hash, with one `account.identity_unlinked { provider, via: "reset" }` row each (0072 §3, amended) — on the user segment, since staff hold none. And the reset link gains a **second, session-minted form**: `POST /user/profile/password/set-link` mints the caller's own `reset_password` token with `payload: { requestedBy: "session" }` and mails §4's own template, and the `reset` that consumes such a token **keeps** the identities, because the session asked and the mailbox confirmed. No new purpose, no new template and no new screen; the anonymous `forgot` is unchanged and still removes them. **Everything else here stands**: the one token mechanism, the templates, verification, the rest of recovery — its three limiters, its `202`, its session sweep and its `account.password_reset` row — the address changes, the invitations and onboarding. Read 0072 §3 for what a reset does to the identities; read this record for the flows.

## Context

[ADR-0066](0066-infrastructure-seams.md) §1 shipped a `MailSender` port with two adapters and Mailpit in the local compose, and said in so many words that the flows are not that record: "password reset, address verification, email change and invitations each need a token table, limiters and their own decisions". Three records have been waiting on those decisions by name. [ADR-0027](0027-credential-self-service-and-reset.md) left "forgot my password" unbuilt because it needs delivery, a single-use token table and the `3 / hour` limiter [ADR-0020](0020-security-baseline.md) reserved a row for. [ADR-0067](0067-organizations.md) §2 mints an invitation's `token_hash` that nothing reads, and its §Consequences records the exposure that address-match acceptance carries while no address is verified — the finding `D15.1` escalated and the operator answered with the token column rather than the gate, on the ground that the gate "is an email flow, D15 ships none". [ADR-0017](0017-identity-model-populations-and-roles.md) gave users a `pending_verification` status and staff an `invited` one, and the auth principals code refuses both at login with a comment that says the flow which clears them is future.

The operator's decision of 2026-09-10 was that Google sign-in ([D19](../plan/0019-google-sign-in.md)) waits for this: a federated identity may only be attached to a local account whose address is verified, and no address is. So this record decides the flows, and it decides them as one deliverable because they share one mechanism — a single-use token, hashed at rest, delivered in a link, consumed once inside the transaction that acts on it — which [ADR-0057](0057-impersonation-sessions.md) §2 already built for the impersonation handoff and which every flow here would otherwise rebuild.

## Decision

### 1. One token table, one mechanism

`account_tokens`: `id`, `purpose` (`verify_email | reset_password | change_email | staff_onboarding`), `principal_type`, `principal_id` (the `sessions` discriminator shape of ADR-0017), `token_hash` (unique; SHA-256 of a `randomToken(32)`, stored only as the hash — the `sessions.refresh_token_hash` form), `payload jsonb` (empty for most purposes; the new address for `change_email`), `expires_at`, `consumed_at`, `ip`, `user_agent`. The token is **strictly single-use**: consumption is `update … where consumed_at is null` returning, inside the consuming transaction, and a refused consumption rolls the stamp back — exactly ADR-0057 §2 and §3's rules, restated here because they are the property every flow below relies on. Minting a token for a purpose **supersedes the live one** for the same principal and purpose (the older row is consumed on the spot), so a person who clicks "resend" holds one valid link, not a growing set. Consumed and expired rows are inert and `db:sessions:prune` grows their deletion, as it did for handoffs.

The organization invitation keeps its own table and its own `token_hash` — ADR-0067 §2 shaped it and it carries organization state a generic row does not — and this record makes it readable (§5).

Every link carries its token in the **URL fragment** (`#token=…`) and lands on a route of the SPA that strikes the fragment from history and posts the token to the API: 0057 §2's argument, inherited whole — no server log, proxy or `Referer` sees it — and 0057's `D13.7` amendment about the profile's history copy, also inherited: the mitigation is the TTL, and each purpose has its own (`VERIFICATION_TOKEN_TTL` 24 h, `PASSWORD_RESET_TOKEN_TTL` 1 h, `EMAIL_CHANGE_TOKEN_TTL` 1 h, `STAFF_ONBOARDING_TOKEN_TTL` 7 d; the invitation keeps `ORGANIZATION_INVITATION_TTL`). The API builds the links, so it learns the two SPA origins: `CUSTOMER_APP_URL` and `ADMIN_APP_URL` enter ADR-0004's schema, defaulting to the local ports and required in production, beside the `VITE_` twins the SPAs already read.

### 2. Templates and their language

Templates are plain functions on the `MailTemplate` shape, one per flow, in the API's mail core; each takes a `locale` and ships **English only**. Where an account exists its stored locale ([ADR-0046](0046-interface-localization.md): a locale is an account fact) is passed; where none does — registration, an invitation to a stranger — the SPA's current locale travels in the request body. The Latvian dictionary is one later step with no signature change, which is what ADR-0066 §1 asked "the first flow that needs a second language" to decide.

### 3. Address verification — an account is usable when its address is proven

Self-registration (`POST /public/auth/register`) creates the account as **`pending_verification`**, mails the link, and answers **`202` with no session** — ADR-0018's "registration creates `active`" is the delta. `POST /public/auth/verify-email { token }` consumes the token, sets `status = active` and `email_verified_at = now()`, and answers **login's body on cookie transport**, the impersonation exchange's shape: the person has just proven the address, and the device the link was opened on is signed in. Login on a `pending_verification` account with the **right** password answers `403 AUTH_EMAIL_UNVERIFIED` — distinguishable, because the caller has already proven the password and ADR-0020 §Enumeration has nothing left to protect; with the wrong one it is the same `AUTH_INVALID_CREDENTIALS` as ever. `POST /public/auth/resend-verification { email }` always answers `202`, never an oracle, behind its own limiter keyed on address and email.

**Amended 2026-09-10 (D18.4), on that step's `security-reviewer`.** The link signs the device in; where that device **already holds a session**, the SPA asks first. The API's answer is untouched — `verify-email` consumes and issues login's body to whoever presents a live token, and the screen still renders in a browser with a session, or the exchange could never run there at all. What changes is that a client must not spend the fragment on mount, because a client that does is a **forced-login primitive** and this token's class is not [ADR-0057](0057-impersonation-sessions.md) §2's. A handoff is minted by an authenticated operator and lives sixty seconds; a verification token is minted by *anyone* who registers an address they control, and it lives a day. So an attacker holds a live link, gets a signed-in person to open it — on the application's own origin, which is no cross-site request for `SameSite` or the transport opt-in header to refuse — and that person then works inside the attacker's account without having typed a password. Signed out, which is the ordinary arrival, nothing changes and the link is spent on sight.

**The invariant this buys — from the moment this section is built, and not before.** *Amended 2026-09-10 (D18.1), on that step's `security-reviewer`.* `D18.1` lands the column and the backfill; the paths that keep the invariant true are this section's own, and they land at `D18.3`. Between the two commits every account created is `active` with an empty stamp, so nothing — not ADR-0067 §5's address-match acceptance, not D19's linking rule — may read `status` as a proof of address until `D18.3` ships. An invariant that is documented before it is enforced is how a claim becomes an account-takeover primitive; the schema's own comment carries the same date. What the section decides is: every `active` user has a proven address — verified by the link, or **asserted** by the staff member who created it through `POST /admin/users` or set it under §7, which stamps `email_verified_at` the same way. Address-match acceptance of an invitation (ADR-0067 §5) therefore closes `D15.1`'s exposure without a new rule, and D19's linking rule reads "active" and nothing else. Accounts that existed before this record are backfilled as verified by migration — they were created under ADR-0018's rule and refusing them would punish a decision that was right when made. **Amended 2026-09-10 (D18.1).** The backfill stamps every pre-existing user **except** one whose status is already `pending_verification`. ADR-0018's rule created `active` accounts, so "existed before this record" and "was created with its address asserted" are the same set for every row registration made; a `pending_verification` row, by contrast, was put in that state deliberately — the `--dev` fixtures carry one — and has proven nothing. Stamping it would make `email_verified_at` disagree with `status` on the one row the flow below exists to move, which is the opposite of the invariant this section buys.

**Amended 2026-09-10 (`D18.5`), on the operator's decision, recording a decision the code had already made.** `verify-email` carries **neither a limiter of its own nor a CSRF guard**, and both absences are decisions rather than omissions — stated here because §6's `confirm-email` and §8's `onboarding/accept` land on the same router and would otherwise inherit the reasoning without anyone re-checking that it still holds.

No limiter, for the reason ADR-0057 §3's exchange carries none: the credential is 32 random bytes, single-use, and guessing it is not a rate problem. The budget belongs at the **mint**, where §4's two limiters now sit, and the global limiter covers the endpoint. No CSRF guard, for the reason `login` carries none (ADR-0029 §CSRF): the request leans on nothing in the browser's jar — the token in the body *is* the credential — so there is no cross-site submission whose intent needs proving. A caller that wants the answer on cookie transport must send `X-Auth-Transport`, which is not a CORS-simple header and therefore forces a preflight against the allow-list, so a cross-site page cannot obtain the cookie form at all.

What would change it: a route on this router that leans on a cookie or acts on an authenticated principal (either one restores something for a CSRF guard to protect); a token minted with less entropy, a longer life, or by a path an attacker can drive at will; or a consume that becomes expensive enough for the endpoint to be worth a limiter as a liveness control rather than as an anti-guessing one. §6 and §8 are to be read against this list where they are built, not assumed by it.

### 4. Password reset — both populations, no session from the link

`POST /{admin,user}/auth/password/forgot { email }` beside each segment's login, unauthenticated, **always `202`** — never an oracle — behind the `3 / hour` per address-and-email limiter ADR-0020 reserved (`RATE_LIMIT_PASSWORD_RESET_MAX`; the window is the limiter's own, one hour, and the rate-limit module grows a per-limiter window for it). A disabled, deleted or `pending_verification` account is mailed nothing and answered the same `202`. `POST /{admin,user}/auth/password/reset { token, newPassword }` consumes the token, sets the hash, **revokes every session of the account** (the person is recovering it from somebody, possibly) and answers `204` **without a session**: the link proved the mailbox, not the device, and signing in is one step away. Both populations get the flow — ADR-0027's "another admin resets you" stays true and stops being the only path — and `super_admin` is no exception, which retires the `pnpm db:seed` recovery path's monopoly without retiring the path.

**Amended 2026-09-10 (`D18.5`), on the operator's decision.** `forgot` carries a **second limiter, keyed on the recipient's mailbox alone** — the email address, with no IP in the key — with a budget of its own, `RATE_LIMIT_MAILBOX_MAX`, over the same hour. The limiter above bounds one *sender* mailing one mailbox and nothing bounded the mailbox itself, so a caller able to vary its source address — a proxy pool, a botnet, or an IPv6 client with a `/64` to walk — could deliver `3 × N` messages into a stranger's inbox with no ceiling at all: ADR-0020's global limiter is keyed on the source address too and varies with it. What the two together decide is one sentence: **a mailbox receives at most `RATE_LIMIT_MAILBOX_MAX` of these messages an hour from everybody, and at most `RATE_LIMIT_PASSWORD_RESET_MAX` of them from any one source.** The mailbox budget is the larger of the two, because a person asking legitimately from a phone and then a laptop is two sources; both are spent by a request the schema goes on to refuse, because a budget an invalid body escapes is not a budget. A request carrying no readable address falls back to a per-source key rather than sharing one bucket, which would be a limiter that stops the whole world at five.

`resend-verification` (§3) keeps its own counter and gains no mailbox limiter here. What it can reach is narrower by construction: a message goes out only for an address that already holds a `pending_verification` account, a set `register`'s own `409` bounds — and registration itself mails an address at most once, because the second attempt is that `409`. The senders §6 and §8 add are authenticated, and their budgets are decided where they are built.

**What this does not close, stated rather than implied.** One sender walking a *list* of distinct addresses is bounded by neither limiter, because both keys vary with the address it names. The only ceiling left is ADR-0020's global `RATE_LIMIT_GLOBAL_MAX / 15 min / IP`, which this repository's committed development profile deliberately raises (ADR-0069) — so on a stock clone the ceiling is looser than the record that set it intended. Closing it takes a **third** limiter keyed on the source address alone, whose budget is a capacity number rather than an anti-abuse one and which therefore has to be sized against real traffic; it is the per-caller-class budget ADR-0020 §Residual risks already carries as an open category, and it is not taken here.

**Amended 2026-09-10 (`D18.7`), on the operator's decision: the third limiter is taken.** The paragraph above stands as the description of the hole and stops being the description of the answer. Every unauthenticated endpoint that mails an address a caller typed — `forgot` on both segments, §3's `resend-verification`, and registration itself — shares **one counter keyed on the source address alone**, `RATE_LIMIT_MAIL_SOURCE_MAX` over the same hour, mounted **beside** the two limiters above and never in place of either. Three keys, three questions, and the three together say: a mailbox receives at most `RATE_LIMIT_MAILBOX_MAX` of these messages an hour from everybody; any one source may mail any one mailbox at most `RATE_LIMIT_PASSWORD_RESET_MAX` of them; and **any one source may mail at most `RATE_LIMIT_MAIL_SOURCE_MAX` of them in total, however many distinct addresses it names** — which is the walk neither of the other keys can see, both of them varying with the address the request carries. The counter is deliberately **one across all three endpoints**: a budget per endpoint would be three budgets for one source to walk in turn, and what is rationed here is this deployment's outbound mail to strangers rather than any one route. Registration is on it because it mails a link to an address the caller typed, which is the same walk under a different verb; closing two doors of three would be a limiter with a hole in the shape of `register`.

**Its budget is a capacity number, and this section said so before it was taken.** The key is the caller's source address, which behind a carrier-grade NAT or a corporate egress is a great many people, so the deployed number is sized against a deployment's own traffic and [`docs/deploy.md`](../deploy.md) says which number to reach for first. The default is set where an honest source cannot reach it — several times over the mailbox budget, and far above what one person asking from a phone and then a laptop produces in an hour — and the committed development profile raises it for the reason ADR-0069 raises the global one: this repository's own browser tier and Postman collection are legitimate senders. A request the schema goes on to refuse is spent, like both budgets above, because a budget an invalid body escapes is not a budget. There is no fallback key to state: this limiter has only the one, and it is the one every request carries.

**What it costs is the collateral the mailbox limiter already carries, one step wider.** A source that exhausts this budget can reach no mail flow at all until the window turns — recovery, verification and registration together — and behind a shared egress that is everybody there. It is accepted on the same ground: recovery by mail is not the only path ([ADR-0027](0027-credential-self-service-and-reset.md)'s administrative reset stands), and the alternative is the ceiling this repository has today, which on a stock clone admits a walk of hundreds of strangers' mailboxes from one source in a window. The senders §5, §6 and §8 add are **authenticated** and are not on this counter; §5's budget is decided where it is built, below.

**Amended 2026-09-10 (`D18.5`), on that step's `security-reviewer`, stating what building §4 cost.** Three things, none of them a change of what the section decides.

**The message is sent off the response path.** Both endpoints that answer `202` whatever they find — this one and §3's `resend-verification` — hand the send to the port without awaiting it. Awaiting an SMTP round trip on the branch where an account exists, against one indexed `SELECT` on the branch where none does, is an account-existence oracle measured in the tens or hundreds of milliseconds, which is larger than the difference [ADR-0018](0018-authentication-sessions-and-tokens.md) §Login erases with `dummyHash()` and is reachable three times an hour per address. Delivery never affected the answer — a failed send is logged and swallowed — so nothing observable changes. **The residual is the mint**, which stays on the path because the link cannot be built without it: one short transaction against a primary key, on the order of a millisecond against a sub-millisecond miss. It is written down here rather than claimed away, which is the posture ADR-0018 §Login takes with its own.

**The mailbox limiter is a denial of recovery, and that is accepted.** Its key is the address with no caller in it, so `RATE_LIMIT_MAILBOX_MAX` unauthenticated requests an hour from anywhere exhaust a known address's budget and the owner cannot obtain a link until the window turns — repeatable indefinitely, and, because one counter serves both segments, a flood aimed at the customer segment locks the same address out of admin recovery. It is inherent to the key: counting only the requests that actually *sent* would reintroduce the oracle the whole section is shaped to avoid, and partitioning the counter by segment would remove the collateral case while leaving the targeted one. It is accepted because recovery by mail is not the only path — [ADR-0027](0027-credential-self-service-and-reset.md)'s administrative reset stands, unaffected and deliberately not retired, so a locked-out operator is reset by another operator and a locked-out customer by staff. The same headers that publish every other limiter's `remaining` publish this one's, so an anonymous caller can also read how much of a stranger's mailbox budget has been spent; it is a small activity oracle on a counter whose whole purpose is to be shared, and it is not worth a per-limiter header policy.

**The reset's own argon2 budget is a counter of its own.** `reset` hashes a password before it can know whether the token is any good, so it takes ADR-0020's login limiter numbers; it must not take that limiter's *counter*, because its body carries no address and its key therefore degrades to the caller's — the same key a `login` with an unreadable address produces. One shared instance would let malformed sign-in attempts from one address block reset **completion** for everybody behind it, which is a denial of the flow a person reaches when nothing else works.

**Amended 2026-09-10 (`D18.7`), on that step's `security-reviewer`: "its key degrades to the caller's" was wrong, and the same sentence had been copied to §5's lookup.** The login limiter's key is the source address **and** an email read out of the *unvalidated* body — which is right where `email` is a field of the schema, and unsafe where it is not. On a body of `{ token, newPassword }` there is no such field, so a caller invents one and every request carries a key of its own. What that costs is smaller than it sounds and is stated rather than inflated (`D18.7`'s `code-reviewer` reading `D18.7`'s `security-reviewer`): both bodies are strict, so an invented field is refused by validation and never reaches the hash or the lookup the budget protects, and every well-formed request keys alike — so the budget on the *work* was never escapable. What escaped was the counting of the refused requests, which is the one thing the key's own rule says must not happen, and what it left behind was an unbounded set of attacker-chosen keys in a counter. Both routes whose credential is an opaque token therefore take a limiter that **reads no body at all** — ADR-0020's numbers on the source alone, one instance each. The decision above is unchanged; what changed is that it is now true of the whole request set rather than of the well-formed part of it.

### 5. Invitations delivered, and accepted by the link

Creating an invitation (`POST /user/orgs/:orgId/invitations`, ADR-0067 §5) **sends** it — the marked call in the organizations service — with a link to the customer app carrying the token ADR-0067 §2 mints. Two reads become real, under the two conditions ADR-0067 §5's amendment stated for them and this record inherits: `POST /public/invitations/lookup { token }` answers the organization's name, the invited address and the role, is rate-limited as an authentication route, and answers one indistinguishable `404` for unknown, consumed, declined and expired; and `POST /user/invitations/accept-by-token { token }`, authenticated, accepts the invitation for the caller **when the caller's address is the invited one** — the token proves the mailbox and the session proves the account, and the two must agree. A stranger who follows the link registers with the address prefilled and locked, verifies (§3), and accepts; an account holder signs in and accepts. Address-match acceptance by id stands as ADR-0067 decided, safe under §3's invariant. Re-sending is revoke-and-create, which the surfaces already have.

**Amended 2026-09-10 (`D18.7`), stating the budget the send at creation takes**, because §4 leaves each authenticated sender to the step that builds it. It takes **the mailbox's own budget of §4, in a counter of its own**: keyed on the invited address with no caller in the key, over §4's hour, and separate from recovery's counter so that being invited never spends the budget an address needs to recover a password.

*This paragraph first said the send needed no budget at all, and that was wrong; the reasoning is left standing rather than deleted, because the shape of the mistake is the useful part.* The argument was that the flow bounds itself — a session, membership, `owner` or `admin`, and ADR-0067 §2 refusing a second **outstanding** offer to one address in one organization. Two things defeat it. That check is pending-only and revoking is the same caller's right, so a repeat costs two requests rather than one. And nothing caps how many organizations an account may create, so "one per organization" is a bound the same caller mints more of at will. What was left was ADR-0020's global limiter — hundreds of messages an hour into an inbox whose owner gave this deployment nothing, each carrying an organization name the sender chose in its subject line. That is the harm §4 built the mailbox key for, reached from an ordinary account.

The key is the **recipient** and not the actor, which is what keeps the honest case whole: onboarding a team is thirty addresses and therefore thirty budgets, where any per-caller number tight enough to bound the abuse would refuse the endpoint's own purpose. The limiter is mounted **after** the membership and impersonation guards rather than first — a stated refinement of ADR-0011's order, in the shape ADR-0057 §1 states its own: what is rationed here is a third party's inbox, so a caller who is going to be refused anyway must not be able to spend it, or the limiter becomes the denial. What remains untaken is a budget on the *actor* — one account walking a list of distinct addresses is still ceilinged by the global limiter alone, and the deterrent is that every row names its `invited_by`. Two residuals belong to ADR-0067 rather than here: an uncapped organization count is now a lever on more than storage, and `organizationNameSchema` admits control characters into a subject line, which today only the mail library neutralises.

The **lookup** is the other half and carries the budget ADR-0067 §5 fixed for it: an authentication route's numbers, in a counter of its own rather than a second mount of login's, so that sign-in noise from one egress cannot refuse its invitees their own link (the argument `D18.5` made for `password/reset`). Its key is the **source alone** and it reads no body, for the reason §4's amendment gives: a limiter that keys on a field this schema does not have is one a caller resets at will.

### 6. A person changes their own address by proving the new one

`POST /user/profile/change-email { newEmail, currentPassword }` — the current password for the reason ADR-0027 requires it on a password change — refuses an address a live account already holds with `USER_EMAIL_TAKEN`, mints a `change_email` token whose payload is the new address, and mails **the new address**; nothing changes until `POST /public/auth/confirm-email { token }` consumes it, re-checks uniqueness inside the transaction, swaps the address and stamps `email_verified_at`. The old address is notified after the fact, plainly, with no link. Sessions are untouched — an address is not a credential. **Refused under impersonation** with `AUTH_IMPERSONATION_REFUSED`: a changed address redirects every future recovery link, which is the definition of takeover-enabling, and ADR-0057 §5's list grows by it — its fourth partial successor.

**Amended 2026-09-10 (`D18.9`), stating the budget this sender takes**, because §4 leaves each authenticated sender to the step that builds it and this is the second one. It takes **the mailbox's own budget of §4, in a counter of its own**: keyed on the recipient — `newEmail`, which is what this body calls it — over §4's hour, and separate from both recovery's counter and the invitation's, so that being asked to confirm an address never spends the budget that address needs to recover a password. The argument is §5's read one flow along: the address is a stranger's, typed by the caller, and the message names it in its body. A session, a password and the supersession of the previous token bound how many *live links* one account holds, and bound nothing at all about how many messages it can send — a repeat is one more request, and each new address is a new mailbox — so the only ceiling without this would be ADR-0020's global limiter, which §5 already refused to accept for the invitation. Mounted **after** the impersonation guard rather than first, on §5's stated refinement of ADR-0011's order: what is rationed is a third party's inbox, so a caller about to be refused must not be able to spend it. The residual is §5's: a budget on the *actor* is still untaken, and one account walking a list of distinct addresses is ceilinged by the global limiter alone — with the deterrent that this sender, unlike §4's, is a named account and every change lands in the audit table.

**Amended 2026-09-10 (`D18.9`): the payload carries the reader's locale beside the address.** §2 says a message to an account is written in that account's stored locale, and the request half has a principal to read one from; the confirmation half is **anonymous** and does not, yet it is what sends the notice to the old address. Carrying the locale in the token's payload is what lets §2 hold on both messages without teaching the anonymous endpoint to read a settings row — one more field in the `payload jsonb` §1 already gave this purpose for the address. A payload with no readable address is a dead link; a locale that is missing or no longer in `LOCALES` falls back to English, which is what every template does with an unknown one anyway.

**Amended 2026-09-10 (`D18.9`), on that step's `security-reviewer`: a pending `change_email` link does not survive a password change.** Every path that writes a new password hash for a user — `profile/change-password`, the mailed `password/reset` of §4, and ADR-0027's administrative `POST /admin/users/:id/password` — supersedes the account's live `change_email` tokens in the same transaction, through the mechanism §1 already has. The reason is the one ADR-0027 gives for revoking every other session there: a password change is what somebody does when the account may be in another person's hands, and a pending address change is a credential of exactly that kind which the session sweep **cannot see**. Without it an attacker who briefly held the password would ask to move the address, wait out the owner's rotation, and confirm the link an hour later — owning the account again from the next `forgot`, with the notice to the old address arriving only after the swap. What is *not* taken here, and is written down rather than left to be rediscovered: the mirror — a live `reset_password` token minted while the old address was current stays redeemable after the address moves — and a notice to the old address at the **mint** rather than only at the confirmation, which would give a legitimate owner the whole TTL to react instead of none.

**Amended 2026-09-10 (`D18.9`): an invitation addressed to the old mailbox is abandoned, and that is a decision rather than an oversight** (that step's `code-reviewer`). ADR-0067 §5 selects an invitation by the account's **current** address, so a change of address leaves any invitation pending for the old one invisible to the inbox and unacceptable — while any pending for the new one become the account's, which is right, because the link that moved the address is proof of that mailbox. The asymmetry is the correct reading of what an invitation is: it is addressed to a mailbox rather than to a person, and somebody who has stopped reading that mailbox has stopped being its invitee. Re-sending is revoke-and-create, which the surfaces already have. Moving the rows with the address was the alternative and is rejected: it would hand an offer made to one mailbox to whatever mailbox the account later proves.

### 7. A super admin sets any account's address

`POST /admin/staff/:id/email` and `POST /admin/users/:id/email`, body `{ email, currentPassword }` — the administrative reset's shape of ADR-0027, the acting operator's own password — behind **`authorize("super_admin")`** at the route, which is the first route to name that role alone (ADR-0019's guard, not a new mechanism; [ADR-0042](0042-client-side-authorization.md)'s policy gains the matching row so the affordance is hidden from everyone else). The address is set immediately and counts as **asserted** (§3): no confirmation link, because the operator is the authority the link would be asking. Uniqueness among live accounts is refused with the population's `*_EMAIL_TAKEN`. A super admin may name their own id; the last-super-admin invariant is untouched because nothing here changes roles or status. Every change — §6's and this one — writes an `account.email_changed` audit row naming actor, subject, old and new address, on ADR-0058's transactional discipline.

**Amended 2026-09-10 (`D18.10`): "asserted" follows `POST /admin/users`'s own split, and stamps nothing on a `pending_verification` row.** §3 names both halves of the invariant in one breath — an address is proven by the link *or* asserted by the staff member who typed it — and `POST /admin/users` has since `D18.3` written the stamp for every status **but** `pending_verification`, on the argument `D18.1` put in §3: a row deliberately left in that state has proven nothing, and stamping it would make `email_verified_at` disagree with `status` on the one status the verification flow exists to move. The same reading has to hold here, or the two paths that assert an address would disagree about what asserting one means. So this endpoint moves the address on such a row — which is the case it exists for, an operator correcting the mailbox a link is about to be resent to — and leaves the stamp null. It writes no `status` on any row: setting an address is not an activation, and a `disabled` account whose address a support case has to correct is squarely inside "any account".

**Amended 2026-09-10 (`D18.10`), on the same argument §6 already carries: an administrative address change spends every live link the account holds.** §6 was amended at `D18.9` to say that every path writing a new password hash spends any pending `change_email` token, because that token is a credential the session sweep cannot see. This path is the other half of the same exposure and is the sharper one: the plausible reason a super admin sets an address by hand is that the mailbox on the row is in the wrong hands, and a link asked for by whoever held it would otherwise undo the correction — with the audit row and the `204` all having happened.

*The step's first draft superseded `change_email` alone, and its `security-reviewer` was right that this was the smaller half.* Every purpose of §1 binds to the **principal id** and none of them binds to an address, so each live row is a link mailed to the mailbox this request exists to stop trusting — and two of them are worse than the one that was closed. `verify_email` activates the row, stamps the **new** address proven and issues a session, which lands exactly on the case this section exists for: an operator correcting the mailbox a verification link is about to be resent to. `reset_password` sets a password on an account this endpoint deliberately does not sign out, and `staff_onboarding` does the same over a seven-day window. So the rule is **an address change spends every link**, iterated over the purpose enum rather than listed, and a purpose that should survive one has to say so where it is declared — which is the safe direction for an omission to point.

What is *not* taken here, and is written down rather than left to be rediscovered:

- **No session is revoked**, because an address is not a credential and a stolen session is the neighbouring endpoint's problem.
- **No notice is sent to the address being left.** Unlike §6 there is no anonymous half to send one from, and the operator making the change is the person who would be telling them. A deployment that wants the notice should read it as §6's template with a different sender, not as a new flow.
- **The route carries no limiter of its own**, so a stolen `super_admin` access token can grind the operator's own password against `403 AUTH_CURRENT_PASSWORD_INVALID` under nothing but ADR-0020's global per-IP budget. That is the residual [ADR-0027](0027-credential-self-service-and-reset.md) §Consequences already records for `POST /admin/{staff,users}/:id/password`, and these two routes join the list — recorded here rather than there because 0027 is frozen. It is the same missing category ADR-0020 §Residual risks names: a per-principal budget for expensive routes.

### 8. Staff onboarding by invitation

`POST /admin/staff` accepts a body **without a password**: the row is created `invited` with a `null` hash (the 0017 delta), an onboarding token is minted and mailed with a link to the **admin** app, and the row is unusable until `POST /admin/auth/onboarding/accept { token, password }` consumes the token, sets the hash and `active`, and answers login's body on cookie transport. A staff member created **with** a password is `active` at once, exactly as today. `POST /admin/staff/:id/onboarding/resend` (admin) re-mints for an `invited` row and nothing else. Login on an `invited` row is refused as any inactive row is.

**Amended 2026-09-10 (`D18.11`), stating the budgets these two senders take** — because §4 leaves each authenticated sender to the step that builds it, and this is the third and last of them. **Both take the mailbox's budget of §4**, keyed on the recipient and in one counter shared between them, because they mail the same message — an invitation to set a password — to the same mailbox, and a counter each would be two budgets for one flow. The keys cannot be the same *shape*, and that is structural rather than a choice: at the create there is no row to name, so the key is the address in the body; at the resend there is no address in the request at all, so the key is the **subject's id**, which stands in for that mailbox one-for-one and, unlike a body field, is matched by the route rather than supplied by the caller. The honest ceiling is therefore `RATE_LIMIT_MAILBOX_MAX` **per key** rather than per mailbox; closing that would mean reading the row's address before the limiter runs, which is a database read on the path a refusal is supposed to make cheap.

*This paragraph first said neither sender needed a budget, and both of its arguments were wrong in the same way — they bounded redirection and identity rather than volume. The reasoning is left standing rather than deleted, because the shape of the mistake is the useful part.* The first was that the create mails an address "at most once, ever", the second attempt being `409 STAFF_EMAIL_TAKEN`. It is not: `DELETE /admin/staff/:id` is a **hard** delete (ADR-0028) and is the same caller's right, so create-delete-create re-mails the same stranger at two requests a message — which is precisely the defeat §5 already recorded for "one outstanding offer per organization", read one flow along. The second was that the resend goes to the address **on the row**, which no field of the request can redirect. That is true, and it is the answer to a different question: it closes *redirection* and says nothing at all about *repetition*, and nothing in the resend dedupes, cools down or counts. What was left was ADR-0020's global limiter — which this repository's own development profile deliberately raises (ADR-0069) — pointed at an inbox whose owner gave this deployment nothing.

What remains untaken is §5's own residual, and it is now the same hole in all four of this record's authenticated senders: a budget on the **actor**. Every one of these counters keys on the recipient, so one operator walking a hundred strangers' addresses gets a hundred fresh budgets. It is narrower here than anywhere else — every message is a row in `staff`, named, audited by `staff_roles.granted_by`, and visible in the panel — and four repetitions is enough that it should be one decision rather than four footnotes.

`onboarding/accept` **does** carry a budget, and it is not a mail budget: it is §4's argon2 control on `password/reset`, applied to the route that is its twin. The request hashes a password before it can know whether the token is any good (ADR-0020 §Hashing cost vs. liveness), so it takes the login limiter's numbers in a counter of its own — its own, for the reason `password/reset`'s is its own: this body carries no address either, so its key degrades to the caller's, which is exactly the key a malformed `login` produces.

**Amended 2026-09-10 (`D18.11`): the language of the two messages is §2's *stranger* seam, not its account seam.** §2 says a message to an account is written in the account's stored locale, and an invited row has none — it is one request old and has never signed in, so there is no settings row to read. That puts it in §2's other case, the one registration and the organization invitation are already in: **the SPA's current locale travels in the request body.** Both `POST /admin/staff` and the resend take an optional `locale`; the create ignores it when a password was supplied, because then nothing is mailed.

**Amended 2026-09-10 (`D18.11`): the accept writes an `account.onboarded` audit row**, on §3's shape for `account.verified` — the actor comes off the row the token claimed, because the route is anonymous and the token is the credential (ADR-0058). It is the staff population's only self-service lifecycle event and the one moment an operator account becomes able to sign in, so it belongs in the trail beside the address change of §7 that can move where that account's future links land.

**Amended 2026-09-10 (`D18.11`), on that step's reviewers: `invited` is a state two neighbouring endpoints had never met, and both had to learn it.** Before this section, `staff.status = 'invited'` was a value ADR-0017 declared and nothing wrote; it is now what an admin creates on purpose, and `password_hash` has been nullable since `D18.1`. Two operations were therefore reachable that produce a row no other path in the module believes in:

- **`POST /admin/staff/:id/password` on an `invited` row** wrote a hash and left the status alone, so the operator was handed a credential that cannot sign in — login refuses every inactive row — and the `204` was a claim nobody could act on. It is refused with `409 STAFF_INVITATION_PENDING`: the invitation is how that account gets its first password, and there is nothing here to replace.
- **`PATCH /admin/staff/:id { status: "active" }` on a row with no hash** produced an `active` operator who can never sign in, and — sharper — one that the last-super-admin count treats as a survivor, because that query reads `status` alone. Refused with the same code, keyed on the **hash** rather than on the transition: moving a row back out of `invited` is legitimate, and a `disabled` row that never onboarded has no password either.

Both are refusals rather than repairs, which is the direction this record already takes with `invited`: the link sets the first password and the status together, and it is the only thing that may. The consume's own statement now says so as well — it matches `password_hash is null` beside the status, so a mailed link can only ever write a **first** password whatever a future guard permits. Two residuals are written down rather than closed: an `active` row may still be patched back to `invited`, which is harmless now but is a nonsense state; and `staff` has no state machine at all — `status` is a free enum field on a `PATCH` while four endpoints reason about it as though the transitions were constrained. A record fixing the legal transitions, and which of them may write a credential, would make these guards checkable instead of remembered.

### 9. What stands

Sessions, rotation, reuse detection, the cookie transport and the CSRF discipline; the mint and every other rule of impersonation; the invitation's shape, its address-match acceptance and its enumeration-safe creation; the current-password rule wherever a password changes; the `MailSender` port and both adapters.

## Consequences

- Every flow is a token row, a template, a public consume endpoint and an SPA route that strikes a fragment — one shape learned once. The cost is the size of the deliverable that learns it; the plan is twelve steps.
- The browser tier reads its links from Mailpit's HTTP API, so `pnpm test:e2e` runs with `MAIL_TRANSPORT=smtp` against the local compose, and the nightly workflow gains Mailpit as a service beside Postgres. A tier that cannot read the mail cannot prove a single flow here.
- Four more limiters' worth of budget accounting in the tier's window floors; `D18.12` re-measures.
- The dev seeds mark their fixtures verified, or the fixtures cannot sign in.
- `docs/specs/` gains a `mail-flows.md` for the token mechanism, verification, reset, address change and onboarding; invitations' delivery joins `organizations.md`; registration's new answer moves in `auth-sessions.md`.

## Alternatives considered

- **Registering an address you do not own — the pre-hijack primitive.** *Added 2026-09-10 (`D18.5`), on the operator's decision: the class was not considered when this record was written, and it is stated here rather than left for somebody to rediscover.* Nothing about §3 stops an attacker registering `victim@example.com` with a password **the attacker chose**. The row sits `pending_verification` and is inert — it cannot sign in, and §4 mails it nothing — so the exposure is not in what the attacker can do next. It is in what the *victim* does: the confirmation link is addressed to them, and following it activates an account whose password somebody else holds. The victim's own click is the takeover. It is worse than it looks in two ways. The victim who later tries to sign up meets a bare `409` (deliberately generic, §Enumeration) and has no way to learn why; and recovery does not rescue them, because `forgot` refuses a `pending_verification` account by design, so the only working button on the page is the one that completes the attack. What closing it would take, in rising order of cost: `forgot` treating a `pending_verification` account as recoverable, which hands the true owner a way back but weakens the "only the link activates" rule; making the link **set** the password rather than only activate the row, which is §8's onboarding shape applied to registration and changes the flow the SPA walks; or the real fix — **creating no account until the mailbox is proven**, carrying the registration in the token's payload and inserting the row at the consume, so there is nothing to hijack and the address stays claimable by whoever proves it first. That last one moves §3's uniqueness check to the consume and needs its own decision about how the pending registration is stored and swept; it is not taken here, and the risk is accepted for now with the reasoning above rather than by silence.
- **Verified after login.** Registration keeps issuing a session, the account is active with an empty `email_verified_at`, and a banner nags. Friendlier for the first minute, and every later flow — invitations, D19's linking, recovery — re-checks a flag instead of leaning on an invariant. Rejected at the operator's decision.
- **One table per purpose.** Four tables with the same five columns and four prune predicates. The `purpose` column is cheaper and the payload column carries the one flow that needs more.
- **The token in a query string** — a link that survives being pasted into anything. Rejected for the reason 0057 §2 rejected it: a credential in a path is a credential in a log.
- **Reset issues a session.** Common, and one step shorter. Rejected because the link proved a mailbox and revoked every session; handing the very next request a session on whatever device opened the mail undoes half of that.
- **Field-level role checks** — `email` accepted on `PATCH /admin/{staff,users}/:id` only for a super admin. A route-level guard is what ADR-0019 gives and what the client policy can mirror; a body field whose acceptance depends on the caller's role is a second authorization mechanism nobody asked for.
